# Wonderweave — First Mystery Concept

**Status:** Proposed  
**Governs:** Early concept exploration for Wonderweave's first complete mystery; this document is not a production specification.  
**Last verified:** 2026-10-10

## Decision sought

Choose a direction for the first mystery before drafting the complete player-facing materials. The current recommendation is **The Unfinished Map**. It is a design hypothesis to test, not owner-approved canon.

## What the first mystery must prove

The first experience should show that Wonderweave is more than a collection of hard puzzles. It should be inviting, beautifully coherent, fair in hindsight, finishable without special equipment, and satisfying for both a curious beginner and a practiced puzzle-solver.

A successful first mystery should:
- make sense as a complete experience without a facilitator;
- offer several genuinely different ways to make progress;
- teach any necessary rule through the experience rather than demand specialist knowledge;
- reward combining observations, not just finding a secret code;
- include a graduated hint path and a satisfying ending;
- work on paper or a simple digital document, with text alternatives for visual or auditory clues;
- avoid required accounts, purchases, location tracking, public disclosure, or travel to real places.

## Concepts considered

### A. The Unfinished Map — recommended

A fictional cartographer left a map whose center is blank. Four small records appear to belong to different subjects—such as a visual pattern, a rhythm, a short text, and a simple arrangement of objects. Each can be explored on its own, and each gives a fair piece of information. Players discover how the pieces relate and use them to complete the map.

**Why it leads:** It gives the experience a strong visual anchor, a tangible goal, several entry points, and a natural reason for different observations to complement one another. It can be atmospheric without relying on a niche historical fact or a real-world location. The map can also become a recurring visual motif if the larger game later earns one.

**Main risk:** The artifacts could feel like unrelated mini-puzzles glued together. The final relationship must be established by clues, not by the designer's authority. The map must not require color vision, hearing, fine motor control, or a particular cultural convention.

### B. The Empty Exhibit

Players investigate a fictional museum display that appears to contain nothing. The surrounding labels, outlines, and descriptions gradually reveal that the apparent absence is itself meaningful.

**Strength:** Memorable framing and strong potential for visual craft.

**Risk:** “Look at the negative space” can become an arbitrary or overly abstract answer. It needs a very precise, testable chain of evidence to feel fair.

### C. The Unfinished Invitation

A message is split across several forms of expression. Players bring different ways of noticing, interpreting, and experimenting to reconstruct what the sender wanted to say.

**Strength:** Warm emotional arc and an explicit welcome to different kinds of players.

**Risk:** The final message may feel predetermined or sentimental unless every fragment has a crisp, independently verifiable role.

## Recommended experience: The Unfinished Map

### Player-facing premise

A mapmaker has left a map with its most important part unfinished. Four records were kept with it. The records look different, and no single one appears to explain the blank center. The player is invited to find what belongs there.

This is fictional framing. It will be presented as a story, not as a claim that a real hidden organization or mysterious person is contacting players.

### Intended emotional arc

1. **Invitation:** A beautiful, legible map with an obvious blank area creates a question immediately.
2. **First foothold:** At least one record offers a small, satisfying observation without requiring prior knowledge.
3. **Growing curiosity:** Other records reward different kinds of attention; players begin to suspect the records share a rule.
4. **Connection:** The map becomes useful, not merely decorative. Players can explain why the pieces fit.
5. **Reveal:** The completed map changes the meaning of the original blank space and makes earlier clues feel more significant.
6. **Closure:** The player receives a complete ending and an optional invitation to continue—not a cliffhanger that withholds the promised payoff.

### Provisional structure

- One main map illustration with a clearly marked blank center.
- Four compact clue records in distinct forms: visual pattern, short text, rhythm represented both visually and verbally, and a simple spatial arrangement.
- One shared relationship that links the records to the map.
- A final assembly or interpretation step that uses evidence from more than one record.
- Three hint levels: a gentle nudge, a pointer to the relevant feature, and a near-explicit step-by-step explanation.
- A solution guide for reviewers, separate from player materials.
- Text descriptions and equivalent non-audio/non-color-dependent versions of any clue.
- A short source and provenance note distinguishing invented elements from any real-world references.

**Important:** These are candidate ingredients, not a locked puzzle list. The exact clues, output, and reveal must be authored and solved end-to-end before the concept is considered sound.

### Craft rules for the eventual puzzle

- Every required inference must have evidence available to the player.
- Each record should contribute to the final result in a distinct, explainable way.
- The central connection should be discoverable; it must not depend on guessing the author's preferred interpretation.
- A hint should restore momentum without shaming the player or giving away more than necessary.
- No clue may depend exclusively on a color distinction, sound, tiny print, speed, specialist trivia, or an inaccessible physical action.
- Solo play must remain satisfying. Cooperation may make the experience richer, but no essential step should require finding strangers online.
- Testers should be able to explain their reasoning, not merely arrive at the intended answer by luck.

## Alternatives and scope discipline

Do not select a software platform, puzzle engine, account system, live map, or community infrastructure for this milestone. A carefully laid-out printable or digital document is enough if it can prove the experience.

Do not add cryptography unless it improves the mystery. Do not add extra puzzle branches simply to inflate puzzle count. One coherent reveal is better than four disconnected tricks.

## Playtest questions

The concept earns the next phase only if playtesting can answer these questions with evidence:

1. Can a new player explain what they are trying to discover?
2. Can players with different strengths make meaningful progress?
3. Can players distinguish a fair challenge from an unclear instruction?
4. Does the relationship among records become visible from the clues?
5. Does the reveal feel surprising in retrospect and fair in hindsight?
6. Can players finish independently, with hints if needed?
7. Are the text alternatives genuinely equivalent rather than second-class versions?
8. Can players describe what was enjoyable, confusing, memorable, or frustrating?

## Next design step

Draft the actual four records and the completed map together, then solve the mystery backward and forward:
- establish the final reveal first;
- ensure every clue uniquely supports it;
- write the hint ladder;
- test for alternate unintended solutions;
- only then polish the narrative and visual presentation.

If that work reveals that the map concept forces the clues, return to the shortlist rather than defending the concept.
