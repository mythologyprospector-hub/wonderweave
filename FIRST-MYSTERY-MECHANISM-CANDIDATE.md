# Wonderweave — First Mystery Mechanism Candidate

**Status:** Proposed  
**Governs:** One candidate puzzle mechanism for *The Unfinished Map*; not a playable or approved puzzle.  
**Last verified:** 2026-10-10

## Design question

Can four small records, each understandable on its own, combine into a satisfying discovery without specialist knowledge, external searching, or a mandatory starting point?

The first attempt used four clockwise relationships to form a loop. This draft joins that structural discovery to a second evidence layer: records of objects moving through a shared center.

## Player-facing introduction

> The mapmaker left a map with an empty center, four unmarked places around it, and four station cards. Four records were found with the map. Each records a relationship between stations and something that happened at the center. You can read them in any order.
>
> Arrange the stations around the ring using the records. Then look again at what happened at the center: what does the evidence suggest about that place?

This asks for a supported interpretation, not a secret word or a single provable motive.

## Map and pieces

The base map has a central space and four identical, unlabeled sockets around a ring. It does **not** pre-place or name the stations. Players receive four separate station cards:

- **LANTERN** — lamp mark
- **RIVER** — wave mark
- **SEED** — seed mark
- **BELL** — bell mark

Players place one card in each socket. The solution is the clockwise order of the four stations, unique up to rotating the completed ring. No socket is marked north. Explain clockwise with a small neutral example; do not rely on color.

Text equivalent: “Arrange the four station cards around four equally spaced positions on a ring. The records specify which station immediately follows another when moving clockwise. The center is empty.”

## The four records — concrete draft

**Record A — Surveyor's sketch**

> **Station note:** River comes immediately after Bell when moving clockwise.
>
> **Dawn, first day:** A piece of chalk lay at the center. I used it to mark a loose stone on the river path, then put it back where I found it.

**Record B — Gardener's field note**

> **Station note:** Moving clockwise from Lantern brings you directly to Seed.
>
> **Midday, first day:** I borrowed the chalk to mark three planting rows and returned it. I left a small bundle of seed packets on the stone shelf at the center.

**Record C — Ferryman's travel log**

> **Station note:** Moving clockwise from Seed brings you directly to Bell.
>
> **Sunset, first day:** I took one seed packet from the bundle to repair a bare patch on the riverbank. I left a coil of spare cord on the stone shelf before heading home.

**Record D — Mapmaker's annotation**

> **Station note:** Moving clockwise from River brings you directly to Lantern.
>
> **Morning, second day:** I used the cord to mark a safe crossing and returned it to the stone shelf. I drew four empty sockets and the paths between them, leaving the center without a label or boundary.

These passages are a coherent draft, not approved final copy. The times establish a readable sequence, but playtesters must still be asked what they infer without being coached.

## Intended solution: structural layer

The four station notes establish these links:

1. Bell → River.
2. Lantern → Seed.
3. Seed → Bell.
4. River → Lantern.

Together they establish the cycle **Lantern → Seed → Bell → River → Lantern**, unique up to rotation. The cards begin unplaced, so the map itself does not give away the ordering. Clockwise is defined by the instruction and neutral example, not by a compass or cultural convention.

This ordering is a short warm-up, not the entire mystery. If it is the only thing players find interesting, the design has not met its ambition.

## Intended solution: evidence layer

The records supply several cross-checkable details:

- Chalk is present at the center, used by the surveyor, then borrowed and returned by the gardener.
- Seed packets are left at the center, then one is taken for a practical use.
- Cord is left at the center, borrowed for a practical use, then returned.
- The records describe objects left in one place, used by different people, and returned there; the mapmaker's drawing leaves the center without a boundary.

Together, these details support a reasonable interpretation: the center functions as a shared-use place where useful things can be left and taken. The mapmaker's choice not to draw a boundary reinforces that reading.

**What is shown:** the station links establish an order; the records describe objects being left, used, borrowed, and returned at the center.

**What is inferred:** the place is intended for shared use.

**What is not proved:** that nobody owns the land, that absolutely everyone is welcome, or that the mapmaker had one exact private motive. The ending must preserve that distinction.

## Does the second layer earn its place?

This remains the key design risk. The object trail may be too explicit or may feel like a moral lesson attached to a simple ordering puzzle. Do not assume it is satisfying just because the logic is consistent.

Cold readers should be able to answer, in their own words:
- What did the station notes establish?
- Which details across different records changed how they understood the center?
- What conclusion is supported, and what remains uncertain?

If they merely repeat “sharing is good,” the second layer has failed. If they identify the cross-record evidence and distinguish evidence from interpretation, it may be worth developing.

## Hint ladder

1. **Gentle nudge:** Use the station notes to arrange the cards. Then compare what each record says happened at the center.
2. **More direct:** Track the chalk, seed packets, and cord across the records.
3. **Nearly there:** The records show useful things being left, taken for a purpose, and returned. What does that suggest about how the center is used?

Hints may direct attention to evidence but must not add a new rule.

## Accessibility requirements

- Provide a legible map and a complete text equivalent.
- Explain clockwise with a simple example; do not rely on color or compass direction.
- Station cards use both distinct marks and readable labels.
- Records can be read in any order, with their times available for checking chronology.
- Players may trace, write, point, or discuss; no timed interaction is required.
- No external search, specialist geography, mythology, cultural knowledge, account, purchase, or real-world activity is required.

## Alternate-solution and consistency checks

Before acceptance, verify:
- each station relationship has one interpretation;
- the four links establish the stated cycle;
- the object movements and times do not contradict each other;
- the records support shared use without overstating ownership or universal access;
- at least two independent record details support the second inference;
- the interpretation is not simply handed to the player by the question or an explicit moral;
- a cold reader can explain both layers without coaching;
- the solution guide separates facts from inference.

## Decision gate

This is a concrete candidate draft, **not a playable or playtested puzzle**. Do not call it canon or production-ready until the map and station cards are assembled as one player-facing artifact, a reproducible solution and accessible equivalents are included, alternate readings are documented, and cold readers can solve it without coaching.

If the evidence cannot support the promised reveal, change the reveal—not the evidence. If the second layer feels attached rather than discovered, redesign it instead of adding decorative complexity.
